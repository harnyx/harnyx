After a paired miss, turn your completed research into a self-contained deep-research report answering the frozen question. The tested answer is the factual conclusion to establish, not the finished report. Preserve the tested question, answer facts, requested components, scope and structured schema. Use the frozen draft's full source/evidence packet and retained research, retrieving public sources as needed to substantiate the report.

Write for a reader who needs to understand and check the conclusion without access to your workspace or private notes. Lead with the result, then develop the evidence and reasoning that establish it. Explain the operative definitions, historical scope and source-selection method when they affect the answer. For a bounded set or comparison, establish the candidate population, apply the conditions consistently, and account for decisive inclusions, exclusions and plausible alternatives. For derived quantities, give the cited inputs and a reproducible calculation; quoting a record at the median does not establish the population count or that median's position. For a global optimum, establish the eligible population and explain the comparison that establishes the optimum. Distinguish what sources explicitly state from your calculations and inferences, and explain material source conflicts or remaining limitations honestly. Organize the report with descriptive sections when they help the reader follow its argument. Depth comes from completing and explaining the necessary research, not a prescribed word count or a diary of searches.

Example of a supported selection report (fictitious evidence, illustrative structure rather than a target length): the question asks which brass objects in a bounded museum collection were acquired before 1920. A good report identifies Aster and Birch as the complete answer, cites the catalogue establishing that Aster, Birch and Cedar are the whole collection [[1]], then cites the material and acquisition entries: Aster is brass, acquired in 1910; Birch is brass, acquired in 1912 [[2]]. It explains that Cedar was acquired in 1911 but is steel, so fails the material condition [[3]]. The citation excerpts contain those actual catalogue statements. A bad report says only "Aster and Birch [[2]]", cites their two entries without establishing the collection's completeness, and puts the selection reasoning into a private note. A report also fails if it invents quotations, hides a material contradiction, or changes the tested answer to make a stronger argument.
